Fast Sounds 
v .............. have 
w ................ wit 
x* ............. box y ................ yes 
z ................ fizz 
ch .............. itch th .............. with sh ............. wish 
Fast sounds are usually the hardest for our students to hear: b, c, d, g, h,j, k, p, qu, t, w, y, and ch. If your student is struggling, avoid fast sounds totally. Then use words with fast sounds only at the end. EventualJy, bring in words that have fast sounds at the beginning. 
Students often attach an /uh/ sound to the 'fast' consonants. You must teach them to clip it offand make JUST the consonant sound. Do NOT let their chin drop down after making the sound. 
Quiet & Vibrating Sounds 
Quiet sounds can be difficult for our students because they are just air. Quiet sounds include: c, f, h, k, p, s, t, ~ th, and sh. Ifyou can feel your throat vibrate while making these sounds, you're adding an /uh/ sound. Clip offthat /uh/ sound. 
By contrast, all vowel sounds are slow and are notquiet-they vibrate. 
Sounds that are both fast and vibrate are usually the most difficult: b, d, g,j, w, and y.

SH and CH sounds 
The main difference between these sounds is how long they last. The /sh/ is slow, like a big ship going across the ocean. The sound continues until you run out ofbreath. But with /ch/, the sound is quick-it is "chopped" off. Use hand gestures to show a ship continuing slowly across the waves for /sh/ versus a chopping gesture for /ch/.

Overview for Tutor Only 
ll1is manual is NOT meant to be only your training tool. Tutors must FIRST watch our tutor training DVDs (included with this level) orelse this manual will not make sense. 
Also, to be successful, tutors must be able to pass our 5-minute Tutor Screening, and a potential student must be able to pass our 10-minute Student Screening. Both ofthose screenings are on our website, www.BartonReading.com . 
During the Intro, you'll explain that good readers and spellers don't memorize. They sound out words. Then you '11 teach your student some tricks so that when they listen to a word, they'll be able to hear all the sounds. 
Then you 'II teach the first skill needed to help your student spell by sounding out. You'll teach him how to listen to a two-sound nonsense word and break it apart into its separate sounds. 
You'11 be working with two types ofnonsense words: VC and CV. VC words have a vowel first, then a consonant. CV words have a consonant first, then a vowel. 
ALL vowels must be pronounced using their SHORT sound. 
Present all ofthe words in the narrow column. Ifyour student can easily do ofthose words,jump to the next step. 
Ifyour student struggled, was quite hesitant, or had difficulty, do NOT go on. Instead, provide more practice using the Extra Practice words at the end ofeach step. Start with the ones labeled Easiest. Once that type ofword is easy for your student, try the ones labeled Hardest. Stay on a step until he has mastered it and can do it easily and accurately. 
You may finish the entire lesson in one sitting. Or you may have to spend several sessions on a single lesson. It doesn't matter. The rule is: stay on a step until your student can do it easily and accurately. Do not rush a student.

Overview for Tutor Only 
As a Pretest 
Do NOT give this as a pretest UNLESS your student has already had recent and intense tutoring by someone using the LiPS program or a different Orton-Gillingham-based system. 
Only in that case should you give this as a pretest to determine ifthe prior tutor taught all seven 
essential phonemic awareness skills well enough that the student does not need this level. 
Ifyou do not know what type ofsystem a prior tutor used, then do not give this as a pretest. 
Instead, start on Lesson I and work your way through all five lessons. 
As a Posttest 
After any student has completed Lesson 5 ofBook 1, you have the option ofgiving the student this posttest. Ifhe has mastered the material taught in Book 1, he should have at least 95% accuracy on every task (maximum of I error per task.) 
Ifyourstudent has more than I error on a task, use the answer sheet to guide you to the lesson that 
needs review. 
You may also use this Posttest as a "regular lesson," a chance for a student to practice and become a bit more solid on his/her phonemic awareness skills before going on to Book 2. 
If you are certain that your student is absolutely solid on everything taught in Book I, and you do not need to prove mastery, you may skip this Posttest and go directly to Book 2.

Philosophy 
This is very hard work for your student. It is important that you correct mistakes in as positive a manner as possible. When a student makes a mistake, please do the foilowing: 
Tell your student what he did right before you teII him what he 
did wrong. 
Accept the blame yourself whenever possible. For instance, if he repeats a word incorrectly, apologize for not saying it clearly, then say the word again. 
Avoid saying things such as, "Here's the right way to do it." A better phrase would be, "Let me show you a trick."

Student Doesn't Break Sounds Apart 
Example: Student repeats word correctly, but during the touch­and-say step, student doesn't/can't break each sound onto a tile. Instead ofsaying each sound separately, the student just says the 
word slowly.

Missing One of the Sounds 
Example: tutor says PLA, student repeats PLA, but during the touch-and-say step, only pulls down two tiles and assigns them the /I/ and /a/ sounds. 
Congratulate the student on the sounds he did identify. Explain there's one more sound at the beginning ofthat word-a very fast sound. 
Have the student watch your mouth. First say the word with the sounds he identified. Then say the original word. Point out that the student can see that your mouth is doing something else at the beginning ofthat word. 
Have your student try moving his mouth in the same shape as that first sound. See ifhe can identify which sound he would be making when his mouth is in that shape . 
Ifthat doesn't work, console your student with the fact that fast sounds are indeed hard to hear. Then make three isolated fast sounds, making sure that one ofthem is the missing sound. Tell your student that one ofthose three sounds is at the beginning of the word. Then say the word again, and see ifyour student can determine which ofthose three fast sounds was at the beginning oftheword.

Other Useful Tips 
Tutor a st11dent at least twice a week, for 45 minutes to an hour 
each time. 
Once a week tutoring is not frequent enough to make progress. 
Tutoring three or four times a week allows a student to progress 
faster. 
Try to end every lesson on success. So ifa student is struggling on a particular step towards the end ofa lesson, back up one step and end on something the student can do well. Or switch 
to playing a game. 
Don't talk too much, especially while the student is trying to do a step in a procedure. This is hard work for your student, and he needs quiet time to process or think about the sounds. 
When you move from one procedure to another, don't explain the new procedure in great detail. Just say something like, "now we'll do the Break-Replace procedure," and start the procedure. Use your hand gestures to guide the student through 
the steps in that new procedure.
