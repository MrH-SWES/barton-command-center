Overvie,v of Each Lesson 
Now that your student has a solid sound-symbol relationship, he'll work with longer one-syllable words. The first four lessons are focused on longer words. By the end ofthe fourth lesson, he'll be reading and spelling words with up to six sounds in them. 
The rest ofthis book is focused on teaching him spelling rules, such as when to spell /k/ with a C, K, or CK, or when to spell /ch/ with CHorTCH. 
He'll also learn two types ofsyllables: Closed syllables and Unit syllables.

Overview for Tutor Only 
No new sounds are taught in this lesson. Instead, your student will start reading and spelling words with four sounds. 
In this lesson, you' 11 introduce the concept ofa Blend. A Blend consists of two consonants ( or two blue tiles) next to each other. Butunlike a Digraph, each consonant makes its own sound. They just 'blend together' nicely. 
It is very important that your student be able to quickly distinguish be~een a Digraph (two letters that makejust one sound-which is why they are both on one tile) and a Blend (two blue tiles next to each other). 
When you putyour tiles in alphabetical order, mix the vowels in with the consonants. But put the digraphs in a separate row above the alphabet. This helps emphasize them and makes them easier to find. 
This lesson will present words that have Blends only at the endof the word-after the vowel. 
During reading, and again during spelling, you' 11 emphasize that the 
letter name Lis made ofup two sounds: /e/ and/I/. 
Sprinkle these questions throughout the lesson: 
Is there a Blend in that word? 
Is that a Digraph or a Blend? 
Where is the Blend? 
How did you know it was a Blend?

Overview for Tutor Only 
No new sounds are taught in this lesson. Instead, your student will 
continue reading and spelling words with four sounds. 
In the last lesson, you introduced the concept ofa Blend. You'II 
continue to work on Blends, but in this lesson, the Blends will be at 
the beginning ofwords. 
Blends at the beginning ofwords frequently have LorRas the 
second Jetter in the blend. L's and R's are the most difficult sounds 
to pronounce correctly. 
Itis very important that your student be able to distinguish quickly 
between a Digraph (two letters that make justone sound-which is 
why they are both on one tile) and a Blend (two blue tiles next to 
each other, each makes its own sound.) 
When you put your tiles in alphabetical order, mix the vowels in with the consonants. But put the digraphs in a separate row above the alphabet. This helps emphasize them and makes them easier to find. 
Sprinkle these questions throughout the lesson after you've taught the newmaterial: 
Is there a Blend in that word? 
Is that a Digraph or a Blend? 
How many sounds does that Digraph/Blend make?

No new sounds are taught in this lesson. Instead, your student will read and spell words with five sounds.

Where is the Blend in that word? 
Is there a Digraph in that word?

Words will now have Blends at both ends, so words will have five sounds.

Overview for Tutor Only 
In this lesson, you' ll reinforce the concept ofClosed syllables, teach the FLOSS spelling 1ule, and introduce students to their second type ofsyllable-the Unit syllable. 
The FLOSS spelling 1ule states that when a word has only one vowel and ends in an F, L, S (the consonants in the word FLOSS) or Z, you must double the last letter. You don'tpronounce the doubled letter; it just has to be there to be spelled correctly. That's why it is a Spelling rule. 
A doubled FLOSS letter is NOT a Blend. In a Blend, each 
consonant makes its own sound. But the extra FLOSS letter is 
silent 
After the FLOSS rule is solid, you will introduce ALL-ourfirst . Unit. Units are groups of3 letters that make an unexpected sound. Units are on red tiles. Units come at the ends ofwords. In ALL, the A is not making its Apple /a/sound. Your student must recognize the sound /all/ as a Unit when spelling and not try to break those sounds apart. 
A word with a Unit is NOT a Closed syllable. (Ifit were Closed, 
the vowel would make its Short sound.) It is a Unit syllable. Mark a Unit syllable by putting a box around the entire unit. 
Ifyou wish, create a Spelling Rules page and write the FLOSS rule on it. Also create a Units section which for now will only contain ALL. Leave that sheet visible throughout the lesson, and review the spelling rules on this sheet at the beginning ofeach Book 3 session. 
Sprinkle these questions: 
Why are there 2 (Fs, Ls, Ss, or Zs) in that word? 
Is there a Blend in that word? 
Is there a Unit in that word? 
SUMMARY: 
• 
Teach the FLOSS rule: if word has one vowel and ends in F, L, S or Z, double the last letter. 

• 
Teach ALL as a Unit. 

• 
Unit syllables are NOT Closed syllables. 

• 
Mark a Unit syllable by putting a box around the Unit. 

• 
Optional: create a Spelling Rules page and list FLOSS as the first spelling rule.

Overview -for Tutor Only 
The first new teaching section ofthis lesson teaches a Reading rule. Your student will learn that C not only says /k/, but also says /s/. The reading rule is: whenever C is followed by E, I, or Y (the Watch-Out vowels) it will say Isl. All the rest ofthe time, it says /k/. 
The second new teaching section deals with a Spelling rule. When 
you hear /kl at the beginning ofa word, how do you know whether to spell it with a Cora K? 
You'11 teach students to use spell /k/ with a C whenever they can. AC cannot make the /k/ sound when it is followed by a Watch Out vowel because the Watch Out vowel switches it to the /s/ sound. If • a C can't make the/kl sound, only then should they spell with a K. 
This requires a modification to the spelling procedure used so far. When spelling a word with the /klsound, the student will now put in a blank tile ( or underline) in that spot and finish spelling the word. After the rest ofthe word is spelled, the student will decide whether to use a Cora K. The rule is: spell /kl with a C whenever you can. 
This is the Kiss the Cat spelling rule. 
Sprinkle these questions through the lesson after you've taught the new material: 
What are the three Watch Out vowels? 
Why did you spell with a C (K) in that word? 
Is that a Closed syllable? a Unit syllable? 
SUMMARY: 
• Teach a Reading rule: Usually C says /k/. But C says Isl when followed by a Watch Out vowel 
(E, I or Y) 
• Teach a Spelling rule: 
Spell /k/ with a C whenever you can. 
When you can't spell lk/ with a C, (because a Watch Out vowel is the next letter,) spell it with a K. 
• This is the Kiss the Cat spelling rule. 
.'. • New Spelling Procedure: Use a blank tile for /kl sound Finish spelling the word Then decide if C or K.
