Overview of This Book 
Your student has probably always been confused about Silent-E, as 
it seems there is no way to know when a silent letter is needed and 
when it is not. 
In this book, you'll teach your student that Silent-E is always there for a reason. And you'll teach the six different reasons for a Silent-E during this book. 
When spelling, your student will learn that ifhe can'tthink ofa 
reason for a Silent-E, he should not add one. During this book, your student will learn new prefixes and suffixes. He will also learn the last Suflix spelling rule-theDropping rule. He must drop a Silent-E when adding a Vowel suffix. He will also drop a Y under two conditions. 
Your student will also learn two more ways to tell a word is Greek, will learn seven Silent-E Units, and will learn a spelling shortcut for TION and SION. 
In the last three lessons, you '11 introduce a new syllable type: Consonant-LE. Inthis type ofsyllable, the Eis silent, but for a very different reason. 
PRONUNCIATION KEY: 
Short Vowels: /ah/= Short A, as in Apple /eh/= Short E, as inEddy fib/ a Short I, as in Itchy /oh/= Short 0, as in Olive /uh/= Short U, as in Upper 
LongVowels: /ail = Long A, ns in Apron lee/=Long E, ns in Teeth /eye/ = Long I as in Idea /oe/ =-Long Oas in Open /ee-oo/ or loo/=two sowids ofLong U. as in Few orTrue 
SUMMARY= 
• 
Silent-E is always there for a reason, but there are 6 reasons for a Silent-E. 

• 
When adding a Vowel suffix, you must drop a Silent-E. 

Under 2 conditions, you also drop a Y. 

• 
Greek words contain PH or Y in the middle ofa word. 

• 
There are 7 Silent-E Units. 

• 
Ifyour student can think ofa I smaller related word, it's easy to figure out whether to use TION or SION. 

• 
Words ending in Consonant-LE also contain a Silent-E. so they also follow the Dropping rule.

Overview for Tutor Only 
In this lesson, you'll introduce Silent-E using one-syllable words. 
InNewTeaching#l,you'll teach thata Silent-E canjump over one letter ( or digraph) and make the first vowel say its own name (its Long sound). You'll contrast Silent-E syllables with Closed syllables so that your student will instantly recognize the difference. 
In NewTeaching #2, you'll remind students that Vowel Teams can also make Long vowel sounds. Your student will learn that homonym pairs often have one word spelled with a Silent-E and the other spelled with a Vowel Team. 
You'll teachyour student that Silent-E's are more common for most long vowel sounds ( except for a Long E), so they'll know the most likely way to spell a word. You'll also encourage them to use their Electronic Spell Checker ifthey're not absolutely sure which way to spell a Long vowel sowid. 
Sprinkle these questions throughout the lesson: 
What kind ofsyllable is that-Silent-E, Closed, orVowel Team? 
Why is that vowel making its Long sound? 
Why is that vowel making its Short soWld?
